%%%%%% Abstract %%%%%%
\begin{abstract}
Unraveling the dynamic causal mechanisms governing large-scale spatiotemporal systems, from atmospheric teleconnections and urban spatiotemporal dynamics to multi-modal infrastructure coupling, is fundamental for scientific understanding and evidence-based intervention. While deep learning has revolutionized spatiotemporal forecasting, extracting interpretable, time-varying causal structures from high-dimensional observational data remains a persistent challenge. Existing causal discovery methods generally treat networks as flat graphs, neglecting the explicit geometric inductive biases inherent in physical space, and struggle to scale beyond hundreds of nodes. Here, we introduce GeoDCD (Geometric Dynamic Causal Discovery), a scalable framework that bridges geometric deep learning with nonlinear causal inference. GeoDCD overcomes the ``curse of dimensionality'' and static assumptions via three innovations: a hierarchical geometric pooling mechanism that exploits spatial coordinates to constrain the causal search space; a gradient-based sensitivity analysis coupled with a Causal Transformer to extract instantaneous, regime-dependent causal graphs; and a near-linear scalable architecture enabling inference on networks with over ten thousand nodes. Validated across domains, including recovering verified atmospheric teleconnections from a 10,512-node global climate grid, tracing spatiotemporal causal dynamics in citywide road networks up to 3,834 sensors, and revealing bidirectional causal coupling between electric-vehicle charging demand and road traffic, GeoDCD offers a transparent, domain-agnostic window into the evolving causal complexity of large-scale dynamic systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Understanding how complex systems evolve and respond to perturbations requires uncovering not only statistical associations but also the underlying causal mechanisms governing interactions among system components. Such systems—ranging from climate dynamics and ecological networks to gene regulation, epidemiology and socio-technical infrastructures—are naturally represented as large-scale dynamic networks with nonlinear feedback, multi-scale dependencies and strong spatio-temporal heterogeneity. As machine learning increasingly serves as a core engine for scientific discovery and decision-making, causal inference in these networks has emerged as a central challenge for building reliable and actionable machine intelligence.

Despite decades of progress, inferring causality in high-dimensional dynamic systems remains notoriously difficult. Classical approaches, including Granger causality and structural equation modelling, rely on restrictive assumptions such as linearity, stationarity or limited dimensionality, which rarely hold in real-world systems. More recent nonlinear methods based on state-space reconstruction, such as convergent cross mapping (CCM), have demonstrated theoretical elegance but remain sensitive to synchrony, indirect effects and noise, often leading to spurious causal links in strongly coupled systems (Sugihara et al., 2012). These limitations become acute when the network scale increases to hundreds or thousands of interacting variables, where computational complexity and error accumulation severely constrain practical applicability.

A series of recent studies has highlighted both the promise and the fragility of existing causal discovery paradigms. Partial cross mapping (PCM) was proposed to explicitly eliminate indirect causal influences by conditioning on candidate mediators, enabling accurate identification of direct causal links in coupled logistic systems, gene regulatory networks, plankton food webs and air-pollution–health interactions (Leng et al., 2020). Similarly, a general model-based framework was introduced to overcome the “curse of synchrony” and indirect effects, successfully recovering predator–prey relations, genetic feedback loops and biomedical causal pathways from observational time series (Ma et al., 2023). These advances significantly improved robustness in nonlinear dynamical settings, yet they remain fundamentally constrained by two factors: dependence on long, high-quality time series and limited scalability to truly high-dimensional networks.

In parallel, causal modelling has been increasingly adopted in Earth system science to evaluate and constrain complex process-based models. Global climate models (GCMs), for instance, constitute the backbone of past and future climate projections, but exhibit substantial inter-model spread, particularly in precipitation and circulation responses. Traditional evaluation metrics based on mean temperature or precipitation patterns may appear accurate due to compensating errors while failing to capture incorrect physical mechanisms. To address this issue, causal model evaluation (CME) was introduced to extract dynamic causal networks from climate data and assess whether models reproduce observed interaction pathways, providing a process-oriented alternative to correlation-based validation (Runge et al., 2020). Related efforts further identified causal gateways and mediators governing the propagation of sea surface temperature anomalies across major ocean basins (Runge et al., 2015), revealing how perturbations originating from regions such as the eastern Pacific or Indonesian seas can influence remote climate components.

However, causal inference in Earth system science exposes an additional fundamental challenge: many key observations are spatially cross-sectional rather than temporally rich. Variables such as soil heavy metal concentrations, population density or ecosystem productivity often exhibit weak temporal variability but strong spatial heterogeneity. Classical time-series-based causal methods, including CCM and Granger causality, become ineffective in such settings. To bridge this gap, geographical convergent cross mapping (GCCM) was proposed to infer causality from spatial snapshots, successfully identifying industrial pollution as the driver of soil copper contamination, environmental controls on population distribution in China, and precipitation–temperature causality on net primary productivity (NPP) in croplands (Zhang et al., 2023). These results underscore that causal mechanisms in complex systems are inherently spatio-temporal and cannot be fully captured by purely temporal or purely statistical models. Beyond environmental systems, causal inference has proven transformative in biology and medicine. Temporal genetic causality tests integrating genetic variation as natural perturbations enabled discrimination between direct and indirect regulatory effects in gene networks (Li et al., 2018). More recently, causal deep learning models have been used to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of over 80 antihyperglycaemic treatment strategies in poorly controlled type-2 diabetes patients, revealing clinically actionable causal effects beyond conventional observational analysis (Schwab et al., 2022). In disaster science, causal Bayesian networks combined with variational inference have quantified dominant drivers of earthquake-induced damage—such as liquefaction versus landslides—directly from satellite imagery, without requiring ground-truth labels (Chen et al., 2022). Together, these studies illustrate the growing importance of causal reasoning for high-stakes decision-making across domains.

Yet, existing approaches still fall short of addressing a unifying methodological problem: how to infer dynamic, state-dependent causal structures in large-scale networks under realistic data constraints. Most current methods either (i) focus on small to medium-sized systems, (ii) assume static causal graphs, or (iii) rely on handcrafted model classes tailored to specific domains. Even recent advances in high-dimensional causal learning using deep neural architectures, such as the D2CL framework combining convolutional and graph neural networks, demonstrate strong performance in recovering causal structures from limited and noisy interventional data, but remain primarily oriented toward static or weakly dynamic settings (Zheng et al., 2023). We argue that causal inference in complex systems must be fundamentally reframed as a geometry-aware and time-varying learning problem. In many natural and engineered networks, interactions are not arbitrary: they are constrained by spatial proximity, physical transport processes, ecological niches or functional modularity. These geometric and topological regularities provide powerful inductive biases that can drastically reduce the effective search space of causal structures. At the same time, causal mechanisms themselves evolve over time, exhibiting regime shifts, nonlinear amplification and transient feedback that cannot be captured by static graphs. Ignoring either geometry or temporal variability leads to causal models that are brittle, uninterpretable or misleading.

In this work, we introduce a unified deep learning framework for causal inference in large-scale dynamic networks that explicitly integrates geometric inductive biases with time-varying causal discovery. By combining spatially structured representations, nonlinear temporal modelling and neural causal estimation, our approach enables scalable recovery of instantaneous and state-dependent causal interactions among thousands of variables. The framework supports counterfactual reasoning and process-level model evaluation, bridging the gap between data-driven prediction and mechanism-oriented understanding. Through systematic experiments on synthetic benchmarks and complex real-world systems, including climate dynamics and ecological networks, we demonstrate that the proposed method disentangles direct from indirect influences, remains robust under strong coupling and noise, and generalizes across spatial and temporal regimes. By advancing from correlation-based learning to geometry-aware dynamic causal inference, this study contributes a methodological foundation for the next generation of scientific machine intelligence—systems that not only predict complex phenomena but also explain, intervene and generalize across scales and domains.

\subsection{A Hierarchical Coordinate-Aware Framework for Scaling Dynamic Causal Discovery}
Unraveling the dynamic causal mechanisms governing large-scale complex systems has historically been constrained by the quadratic search complexity $O(N^2)$ of flat graph architectures, which generates massive spurious connections and limits scalability. Here, we introduce GeoDCD, a framework designed to scale dynamic causal discovery to high-dimensional spatiotemporal systems by integrating explicit coordinate-space geometric inductive biases.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}, GeoDCD bypasses the curse of dimensionality by incorporating a hierarchical geometric pooling mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}b,c). Instead of treating variables as exchangeable nodes in a flat graph, the framework leverages coordinate inputs to aggregate spatially proximal sensors or grid cells into localized patches (Level 1) and macro-regions (Level 2). This coordinate-driven aggregation restricts the causal search space to near-linear complexity ($O(kN \log N)$), effectively filtering out spatially incoherent false-positive links. The pooled representations are then encoded via a Causal Transformer, and dynamic causal strength matrices are extracted at multiple scales through Jacobian sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}d). This architecture enables transparent, domain-agnostic causal discovery across diverse physical and engineered domains (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}e).

\subsection{Discovering Atmospheric Teleconnections and Regime Shifts on Global Grids}
Global atmospheric teleconnections represent long-range causal couplings mediated by planetary Rossby waves. Analyzing these teleconnections has traditionally been limited to small-scale regional grids due to high computational overhead. We applied GeoDCD to ERA5 reanalysis sea-level pressure (SLP) gridded data at $2.5^\circ \times 2.5^\circ$ resolution, yielding a global network of $N=10{,}512$ nodes, constituting one of the largest data-driven causal teleconnection analyses to date.

\textbf{Emergence of Global Causal Gateways.}
Without any climatological supervision, GeoDCD's inferred global causal network identifies the strongest causal gateway spontaneously near $45^\circ\text{S}$, $72^\circ\text{E}$ in the Southern Indian Ocean (Fig.~\ref{fig:climate_multi}a). The associated directed causal arcs propagate eastward and northward, tracing the canonical corridors of the Roaring Forties and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. This discovered pathway is highly consistent with atmospheric wave-guide dynamics, where pressure perturbations propagate across planetary boundary corridors to modulate weather patterns in downstream targets, including Western Australia, South Africa, and Patagonia.

\textbf{Geometric Pooling Recovers Atmospheric Zonal Structures.}
The hierarchical geometric pooling naturally aggregates the 10,512-node grid into coherent spatial clusters (Fig.~\ref{fig:climate_multi}b). Strikingly, these unsupervised spatial units reconstruct the planet's zonal pressure belts, including the subtropical high-pressure ridges and subpolar low-pressure systems. In the Northern Hemisphere, the model identifies a dense, interconnected network of continental hubs spanning North America, Siberia, and Europe, validating the coupled dynamics of the Siberian High and the North Atlantic Oscillation.

\textbf{Non-linear Regime-Dependent Causal Shifts.}
A key capability of GeoDCD is capturing how causal topologies reorganize across different climatological states. We compared the dynamically inferred causal networks during El Niño and La Niña episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:climate_multi}c). During El Niño phases, the tropical Pacific emerges as the dominant causal driver, with prominent eastward arcs transmitting pressure anomalies to the western Americas. During La Niña, this equatorial dominance diminishes, and the causal topology shifts toward enhanced subtropical coupling. This spontaneous recovery of atmospheric bridge dynamics from raw SLP observations confirms that the framework captures genuine state-dependent causal shifts in the climate system.

\subsection{Decoupling Cross-Modal Causal Feedbacks in Urban Infrastructure Networks}
Real-world urban systems are characterized by tightly coupled infrastructure subsystems whose interactions yield complex feedback loops. To evaluate GeoDCD's performance in multi-modal environments, we deployed the framework on the ST-EVCDP dataset covering Shenzhen, China. We constructed a dual-modality network of $N=494$ nodes by pairing road traffic occupancy and electric vehicle (EV) charging energy volume across 247 traffic analysis zones (TAZs), with shared geographic coordinates serving as the geometric prior.

\textbf{Asymmetric Bidirectional Coupling.}
As visualized in Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_modal_multi}a, GeoDCD successfully decouples Traffic $\to$ Energy and Energy $\to$ Traffic causal links overlaying the physical road network. The cumulative causal impact exhibits a striking spatial asymmetry (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_modal_multi}c,d). Major energy-to-traffic causal hubs (where charging load fluctuations trigger local traffic anomalies) cluster in the high-density western urban core (Nanshan and Futian districts). Conversely, dominant traffic-to-energy drivers (where elevated road occupancy redirects downstream charging behaviors) populate the eastern transportation corridors (Luohu and Longgang). This spatial segregation reveals distinct functional zones that operate either as traffic-driven or energy-driven subsystems.

\textbf{Diurnal Temporal Phase Transitions.}
The interaction strength between transport and grid subsystems is highly non-stationary. GeoDCD tracks the diurnal evolution of cross-modal coupling across consecutive inference windows (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_modal_multi}b). The dominant causal direction reverses periodically, showing crossover points aligned with morning and evening peak hours. Traffic-to-energy coupling intensifies during commuter rushes, whereas energy-to-traffic spillover dominates during midday and late evening charging surges. This temporal fluctuation confirms that urban inter-modal dependencies are characterized by rapid regime transitions, which are lost under static network formulations.

\subsection{Real-Time Causal Tracing of Transient Dynamics in City-Scale Road Networks}
To demonstrate scalability and physical consistency across engineered networks, we deployed GeoDCD on citywide traffic datasets of increasing magnitude: Beijing ($N=513$), San Diego ($N=716$), Greater Bay Area ($N=2{,}352$), and Greater Los Angeles ($N=3{,}834$).

\textbf{Near-Linear Computational Scalability.}
Traditional deep causal discovery methods scale quadratically, making end-to-end inference on networks exceeding 1,000 nodes computationally prohibitive. In contrast, GeoDCD exhibits a near-linear computational complexity curve ($R^2 = 0.992$) as the number of nodes increases (Fig.~\ref{fig:urban_multi}a). The training process for the mega-scale GLA network ($N=3{,}834$) was completed in 4.2 hours with peak memory usage restricted under 16GB. This efficiency is achieved by the hierarchical geometric pooling (Fig.~\ref{fig:urban_multi}b), which reduces the effective sequence interactions within the causal attention heads.

\textbf{Temporally Structured Causal Dynamics.}
In the Beijing (BJ) network, the dynamically inferred causal intensities reveal distinct, physically interpretable spatiotemporal patterns (Fig.~\ref{fig:urban_multi}c). At midnight, the network is highly sparse and dominated by self-loops, reflecting weak inter-node interactions during low-activity periods. During the morning rush hour, a centripetal causal pattern emerges, with directed links converging from outer ring-road interchanges toward the urban core, consistent with the known geographic structure of commuter inflows. During the evening rush hour, the directionality reverses, exhibiting a centrifugal pattern as causal influence radiates outward along arterial corridors. This systematic temporal reversal of causal directionality which reproduced without any domain-specific priors demonstrates that GeoDCD recovers physically meaningful interaction patterns directly from observational time series.

\subsection{Physical Validation of Geometric Priors under Controlled Chaotic Dynamics}
While evaluating GeoDCD on real-world geophysical and urban networks demonstrates physical plausibility, these systems lack a definitive, mathematical ground truth. To establish rigorous, quantitative validation of the framework's topology recovery and the efficacy of its geometric priors, we conducted controlled experiments on four synthetic benchmarks with known structural connectivity: VAR, Lorenz-96, Cluster-Lorenz, and Finance. The spatial coordinate distributions defining the underlying manifolds for these benchmarks are visualized in Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}a--d.

\textbf{Topological Recovery under Non-linear and Chaotic Regimes.}
In chaotic systems governed by non-linear differential equations, such as the Lorenz-96 model (where variables are coupled along a circular manifold, Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}b), traditional linear Granger causality methods (e.g., GVAR) fail to capture the true feedback loops (F1-Score: 0.42). While deep learning baselines (e.g., CUTS+, Causalformer) improve recovery by modeling non-linearities, they lack geometric constraints, generating numerous spurious connections. GeoDCD achieves superior topological reconstruction, obtaining an F1-Score of \textbf{0.99} and restricting the Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) to \textbf{9.6} (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}e). This represents a \textbf{36.8\% reduction in SHD} compared to the runner-up method (Causalformer, SHD: 15.2), confirming that coordinate-space inductive biases successfully constrain the non-linear search space under chaotic regimes.

\textbf{Distinguishing Multi-Scale Couplings.}
This advantage is further highlighted in the Cluster-Lorenz dataset, which simulates localized spatial patches characterized by dense intra-cluster and sparse inter-cluster links (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}c). While flat domain-agnostic models struggle to isolate regional interactions, GeoDCD's hierarchical geometric pooling leverages the spatial manifold to aggregate localized dynamics. Consequently, it achieves a high recovery precision (F1-Score: \textbf{0.95}, Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}e), outperforming models that attempt to infer group structure implicitly from time series alone.

\textbf{Robustness against Adversarial Coordinate Priors.}
A critical risk of integrating geometric priors is the potential to overfit to uninformative or misleading spatial coordinates. To stress-test the model's resilience, we evaluated it on the Finance dataset ($N=40$), where variables have no physical spatial layout. We assigned random virtual coordinates to each variable, treating the geometric prior as adversarial noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_validation_multi}d). Despite this uninformative spatial guidance, GeoDCD achieved an F1-Score of \textbf{0.78} and an AUROC of \textbf{0.96}, significantly outperforming flat models (Causalformer F1: 0.72; CUTS+ F1: 0.70). This result suggests that GeoDCD operates as a dual-mode learner: it exploits geometric manifolds when informative, but successfully transitions to extracting intrinsic topology solely from temporal dynamics when spatial signals are uninformative, preventing performance degradation from erroneous coordinate inputs. Comprehensive ablation metrics, dataset equations, and parameter details are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The present work establishes geometry-aware dynamic causal discovery (GeoDCD) as a principled framework for inferring time-varying causal structures in large-scale spatiotemporal networks. By integrating explicit spatial inductive biases with neural Granger causality, GeoDCD addresses two fundamental limitations that have constrained prior approaches: the curse of dimensionality, which renders $O(N^2)$ causal search intractable beyond hundreds of nodes, and the static-graph assumption, which obscures the regime-dependent nature of physical interactions. The Hierarchical Geometric Pooling mechanism reduces effective interaction length through coordinate-driven aggregation, while Jacobian-based dynamic inference extracts instantaneous causal strength tensors that resolve transient mechanisms, such as transient causal dynamics in road networks and atmospheric teleconnection pathways, which are irretrievably lost under temporal averaging.

Importantly, while state-of-the-art climate foundation models (e.g., GraphCast, Pangu-Weather) achieve unprecedented forecasting accuracy, they function as black-box predictors of meteorological states. In contrast, GeoDCD does not aim to compete in forecasting metrics; rather, it uses predictable variance as a mathematical vehicle to reconstruct the underlying physical causal network, offering a transparent, interpretable lens into Earth system teleconnections and dynamical regime shifts. Furthermore, unlike classic constraint-based methods (such as PCMCI) that rely on rigorous, computationally expensive conditional independence tests, which limit their scalability to small-scale networks and assume temporal stationarity, GeoDCD uncovers instantaneous, state-dependent causal graphs at a near-linear computational complexity, processing networks with over ten thousand nodes.

The cross-domain evidence presented here substantiates GeoDCD's generality. On synthetic benchmarks, near-perfect structural recovery is achieved while robustness is maintained when spatial coordinates are adversarially corrupted (F1: 0.78 under random virtual coordinates). On a 10,512-node global SLP grid, which represents the largest data-driven causal teleconnection analysis to date, the framework spontaneously recovers the Southern Annular Mode gateway, Rossby wave propagation corridors and ENSO regime-dependent topology shifts, all consistent with established atmospheric science. On city-scale road networks up to $N=3{,}834$ nodes, GeoDCD outperforms state-of-the-art spatio-temporal baselines while exhibiting near-linear computational scaling. Finally, the cross-modal experiment on Shenzhen's coupled EV charging--traffic system demonstrates that GeoDCD discovers bidirectional causal feedbacks between energy and transport subsystems without any architectural modification, revealing spatially differentiated ``energy-dominant'' and ``traffic-dominant'' functional zones that carry direct implications for urban infrastructure planning.

\textbf{Limitations and scope.}
Several limitations warrant attention. First, the quality of Hierarchical Geometric Pooling depends on the assumption that spatial proximity is a meaningful proxy for causal coupling; in domains where functional connectivity diverges from physical distance (e.g., air traffic, financial contagion), the pooling topology must be adapted or learned jointly with the graph structure. Second, while our basis decomposition reduces parameters substantially, the choice of $N_{\mathrm{basis}}$ introduces a hyperparameter that governs the trade-off between expressivity and sparsity; insufficient bases may underfit complex topologies, whereas excessive bases risk overfitting on limited data. Third, Jacobian-based inference is computationally intensive at scale: computing full $(N \times N)$ sensitivity tensors requires $N$ forward-backward passes per time step, which we mitigate via basis decomposition but cannot eliminate entirely. Fourth, the climate experiment reveals that many atmospheric drivers are inherently zonal (i.e., stretching along entire latitude circles without localised structure), causing K-Means clustering to produce geographically degenerate clusters; advanced spatial decomposition methods that decouple geometric proximity from zonal band structure may better accommodate such anisotropic domains. Fifth, in multi-modal settings, disparities in sampling rate, signal-to-noise ratio and dynamic range across modalities may bias the estimated causal strengths; explicit normalisation or modality-aware weighting schemes warrant further investigation.

\textbf{Pathways to application.}
Translating GeoDCD from research demonstrations to operational use requires several developments. (i) \textit{Multi-modal integration:} the Shenzhen cross-modal experiment demonstrates zero-shot transfer to dual-modality systems; extending this to three or more coupled subsystems, such as energy, transport, and land-use simultaneously, would test whether geometric priors remain effective in increasingly heterogeneous feature spaces. (ii) \textit{Continuous-time extensions:} current GeoDCD operates on discrete time steps; extending the framework to continuous-time dynamical systems via neural ordinary differential equations would enable irregularly sampled inference and finer temporal resolution of causal mechanisms. (iii) \textit{Online adaptation:} deploying GeoDCD in production environments demands streaming or online learning capabilities that adapt causal graphs in response to distribution shifts without full retraining; incorporating Bayesian uncertainty quantification into the dynamic inference module would provide calibrated confidence estimates for each inferred link. (iv) \textit{Broader domain evaluation:} while we demonstrate applicability to climate grids, urban road networks and cross-modal energy-transport systems, systematic evaluation on additional physical domains, including power grid stability, ecological food webs, and neuronal circuit analysis, is essential to consolidate GeoDCD as a truly domain-agnostic causal discovery framework.

Collectively, these results position geometry-aware neural causal inference as a viable paradigm for scientific machine learning: one that moves beyond correlation-based prediction toward mechanistic understanding, enabling not only accurate forecasting but also uncovering the latent dynamic causal pathways that drive complex system transitions.

\section{Preliminary}
\label{sec:preliminary}

This section establishes the theoretical underpinnings of our study by tracing the evolution of Granger Causality from linear formulations to neural interpretations. We subsequently provide a formal definition of the multivariate spatiotemporal causal discovery problem, grounded in geometric coordinates.

\subsection{Granger Causality: Linear and Neural Views}

\paragraph{Linear Granger Causality}
The core tenet of Granger Causality lies in \textit{predictability}. For two time-series variables $X_j$ and $X_i$, $X_j$ is said to Granger-cause $X_i$ ($X_j \to X_i$) if the inclusion of $X_j$'s history significantly reduces the prediction error for $X_i$'s future values, compared to using the history of $X_i$ alone.

In classical Vector Autoregression (VAR) models, this relationship is formalized as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^P \mathbf{A}_\tau \mathbf{X}_{t-\tau} + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation}
where $P$ denotes the maximum time lag, and $\mathbf{A}_\tau$ represents the coefficient matrix at lag $\tau$. If the element $A_{\tau, ij} = 0$ for all $\tau \in \{1, \dots, P\}$, we conclude that $X_j \nrightarrow X_i$.

However, this linear formulation faces two significant impediments when applied to large-scale geospatial systems ($N \sim 10^3$). First, the parameter space expands quadratically ($O(N^2)$), rendering computation intractable. Second, linear assumptions fail to capture the complex, nonlinear dynamics inherent in physical phenomena, such as fluid turbulence or nonlinear climatic interactions.

\paragraph{Neural Granger Causality}
To surmount the limitations of linearity, Neural Granger Causality (Neural GC) replaces linear transformations with nonlinear deep neural networks, denoted as $f_\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_{t-1}, \dots, \mathbf{X}_{t-P}) + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation}
In this framework, causal inference does not rely on fixed coefficient matrices. Instead, causality is estimated by examining how perturbations in the input history influence the model output. Jacobian-based sensitivity analysis is the prevailing method for quantifying this influence:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}_{i\leftarrow j}(t) = \left| \frac{\partial \hat{x}_{i, t+1}}{\partial x_{j, t}} \right|
\end{equation}
GeoDCD operates within this Neural GC paradigm, specifically adapted and optimized for high-dimensional geometric spatiotemporal data.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
We consider a multivariate spatiotemporal system comprising $N$ spatial locations. The available data consists of two distinct components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation Matrix} $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N}$: Here, $T$ denotes the total time steps and $N$ the number of spatial nodes. At time $t$, the system state vector is denoted as $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{D}_{t, :} \in \mathbb{R}^N$.
    \item \textbf{Coordinate Matrix} $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$ (or $\mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$): This contains the physical positioning of each variable (e.g., latitude/longitude or Cartesian coordinates), providing the model with critical geometric priors.
\end{itemize}

Our objective is to leverage $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{C}$ to infer latent causal structures, specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Causal Structure} $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$: The directed connectivity skeleton representing persistent interactions that do not vanish over time.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Causal Strength} $\mathcal{S}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad t=1, \dots, T$: A metric capturing the instantaneous intensity of interactions as the system state evolves.
\end{enumerate}

Geospatial systems are typically characterized by high dimensionality ($N \gg T$), strong spatial autocorrelation, and non-stationarity. GeoDCD addresses these challenges through geometrically aware hierarchical modeling.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Overall Workflow Architecture}
The GeoDCD framework operates as a unified, end-to-end deep learning pipeline. It accepts raw multivariate time-series data and their associated spatial coordinates as input and produces both a static causal graph (representing long-term structural dependencies) and a dynamic causal tensor (representing instantaneous interaction strengths). The methodology is structured into five sequential components, each addressing a specific aspect of the spatiotemporal causal discovery problem.
The pipeline is composed of five sequential components: (1) \textbf{Spatiotemporal Data Ingestion}, which handles preprocessing and tensor construction; (2) \textbf{Hierarchical Geometric Pooling}, performing bottom-up spatial aggregation; (3) \textbf{Geo-Encoder Backbone}, modeling latent dynamics via Transformers; (4) \textbf{Sparse Structural Learning}, estimating the adjacency matrix via Basis Decomposition; and (5) \textbf{Dynamic Inference \& Jacobian Analysis}, extracting time-varying causal graphs.

\subsection{Spatiotemporal Data Ingestion and Preprocessing}
The foundation of the pipeline is the rigorous preparation of data tensors. We employ a sliding window strategy to model temporal dependencies. The multivariate time-series $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{total} \times N}$ is sliced into overlapping sequences of length $L$, transforming the dataset into a tensor $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N \times L}$. This allows the model to learn from local temporal contexts.
To facilitate stable training and geometric clustering, we apply Z-score normalization (standardization) to both the time-series data and the spatial coordinates $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$. Normalizing coordinates ensures that the clustering process is invariant to the absolute scale of the physical system.

\subsection{Hierarchical Geometric Pooling}
To resolve the $O(N^2)$ scalability bottleneck, GeoDCD introduces a Geometric Pooler. This component uses physical proximity to aggregate nodes into ``super-nodes'' or patches, creating a hierarchical representation of the system. This allows the causal discovery process to occur at multiple resolutions, coarse-grained for regional trends and fine-grained for local interactions.

\subsubsection{K-Means Spatial Clustering}
We employ the K-Means algorithm on the normalized coordinate matrix $\mathbf{C}$ to partition the $N$ nodes into $K$ distinct clusters. This step injects strong inductive bias: we assume that spatially proximal nodes are more likely to share functional characteristics or be governed by similar local dynamics.
The algorithm minimizes the intra-cluster variance:
\begin{equation}
\arg \min_{\mathbf{S}} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in \text{Cluster}_k} || \mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{\mu}_k ||^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{S} \in \{0, 1\}^{N \times K}$ is the hard assignment matrix derived from the clustering labels. Specifically, $S_{ik} = 1$ if node $i$ is assigned to cluster $k$, and 0 otherwise. This matrix is precomputed during the initialization phase.

\subsubsection{Feature Aggregation (Pooling)}
During the forward pass, the feature representations of nodes within a cluster are aggregated to form the input for the next level of the hierarchy. The pooling operation is defined as a matrix multiplication:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}^{(l+1)} = (\mathbf{S}^{(l)})^\top \mathbf{X}^{(l)}
\end{equation}
To prevent magnitude explosion, the assignment matrix $\mathbf{S}$ is column-normalized, effectively computing the average feature vector for each cluster. This reduces the spatial dimension from $N$ to $K$, significantly lowering the computational cost for subsequent layers. The coordinates for the new level are similarly updated by computing the centroids of the clusters: $\mathbf{C}^{(l+1)} = (\mathbf{S}^{(l)})^\top \mathbf{C}^{(l)}$. To enhance robustness and prevent overfitting, we apply data augmentation during training by adding Gaussian noise to the normalized coordinates before clustering:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_i^{aug} = \mathbf{c}_i + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the shift scale parameter.

\subsection{Geo-Encoder Backbone}
At the heart of GeoDCD is a sequence-to-sequence architecture that models the temporal evolution of the system. We utilize a Causal Transformer, adapted from the standard Transformer architecture, to capture long-range temporal dependencies without suffering from the vanishing gradient problem common in Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).
\subsubsection{Positional Encoding}
Since Transformers are permutation-invariant, we must inject explicit temporal order information. We use sinusoidal positional encodings added to the input embeddings:
\begin{align}
PE_{(pos, 2i)} &= \sin(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{model}}) \\
PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} &= \cos(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{model}})
\end{align}
This allows the model to distinguish between different time steps within the look-back window.
\subsubsection{Causal Self-Attention}
The core mechanism is the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA). To ensure that the prediction for time $t$ depends only on the history $t' \le t$ (respecting physical causality), we apply a Causal Mask to the attention scores.
The attention mechanism is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \mathbf{M}\right)\mathbf{V}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{M}$ is a square matrix where $M_{ij} = -\infty$ if $j > i$ (masking future positions) and $0$ otherwise. This strictly prevents information leakage from the future.
\subsubsection{Pre-LayerNorm Architecture}
To improve training stability and convergence, we adopt the \textbf{Pre-LayerNorm} architecture. Unlike the original Post-LN design where Layer Normalization (LN) is applied after the residual connection, we apply LN before the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) and Feed-Forward Networks (FFN). This structural modification creates a direct path for gradients to flow through the residual connections, significantly mitigating the risk of gradient vanishing in deep networks.
The forward pass for a single encoder layer is defined as:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{Z}' &= \text{MSA}(\text{LN}(\mathbf{Z})) + \mathbf{Z} \\
\mathbf{Z}_{out} &= \text{FFN}(\text{LN}(\mathbf{Z}')) + \mathbf{Z}'
\end{align}
This design mitigates the risk of gradient vanishing in deep networks. The architecture consists of a temporal encoder with a prediction head:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Encoder}: Maps the input sequence $\mathbf{X}$ to a latent dynamic representation $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N \times L \times D_{model}}$.
    \item \textbf{Prediction Head}: Forecasts the next time step directly from the encoded representations:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{W}_{pred} \mathbf{z}_{t} + \mathbf{b}_{pred}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{W}_{pred}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{pred}$ are the parameters of the linear prediction head.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Sparse Structural Learning via Basis Decomposition}
A standard dense layer connecting $N$ nodes would require $N^2$ parameters, which is prohibitive. GeoDCD implements a novel Causal Graph Layer that learns the adjacency matrix efficiently.
\subsubsection{Basis Decomposition}
Instead of learning a unique weight for every pair of nodes directly, we decompose the weight tensor into a linear combination of a small number of shared basis matrices. Let $N_{basis}$ be the number of bases (e.g., 4) and $D_{model}$ be the channel dimension.
We define:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{basis} \times N \times N}$: The tensor of basis adjacency matrices.
    \item $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{model} \times N_{basis}}$: The coefficient matrix determining how channels mix the bases.
\end{itemize}
The effective weight matrix for the graph convolution is reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W}_{eff} = \sum_{k=1}^{N_{basis}} \mathbf{c}_{\cdot, k} \otimes \mathbf{B}_{k}
\end{equation}
This reduces the parameter space significantly, acting as a low-rank approximation of the causal structure. It forces the model to learn fundamental interaction patterns that are reused across different feature channels.
\subsubsection{Graph Propagation and Loss Functions}
The latent representation $\mathbf{Z}$ is propagated through this learned structure:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}_{next} = \tanh(\mathbf{Z} \cdot \mathbf{W}_{eff})
\end{equation}
The training is guided by a composite loss function $\mathcal{L}_{total}$ that balances prediction accuracy and structural sparsity:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{total} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t} ||x_{t+1} - \hat{x}_{t+1}^{pred}||^2}_{\text{Prediction Loss}} + \lambda_{L1} \underbrace{||\mathbf{A}_{learned}||_1}_{\text{Sparsity Loss}}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prediction Loss}: Directly implements the Neural Granger Causality principle. If node $j$ causes node $i$, the link $A_{ji}$ must be non-zero to minimize the prediction error of $x_{i, t+1}$.
    \item \textbf{Sparsity Loss}: An L1 penalty on the adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A}$ promotes a sparse graph, reflecting the assumption that physical systems are locally connected and not fully dense.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Dynamic Inference and Jacobian Analysis}
While the learned matrix $\mathbf{A}$ represents the ``static'' or average structural connectivity, real-world systems exhibit dynamic causality where interaction strengths fluctuate over time. GeoDCD extracts these dynamics during the inference phase.
\subsubsection{Jacobian Sensitivity Analysis}
To quantify the instantaneous causal strength from node $j$ to node $i$ at time $t$, we compute the Jacobian of the predicted future state with respect to the input state.
Let $\hat{x}_{i, t+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_t)$ be the model's prediction. The dynamic causal strength is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{i \leftarrow j}(t) = \left\| \frac{\partial \hat{x}_{i, t+1}}{\partial x_{j, t}} \right\|_F
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm, aggregating the sensitivity across the feature dimension. We compute this via automatic differentiation, iterating through each target node $i$ to obtain the gradient of its prediction with respect to the input tensor $\mathbf{X}_t$. This yields a dynamic adjacency tensor of shape $(N \times N \times T)$, capturing the time-varying causal topology.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
Our evaluation is structured around three complementary objectives: (i) recovering causal structures in controlled synthetic settings with known ground-truth topologies; (ii) validating physical plausibility on a global climate grid where teleconnection mechanisms are well established; and (iii) demonstrating scalability and cross-modal generality on real-world urban networks.

\paragraph{Synthetic benchmarks.}
We employ four benchmark suites of increasing complexity. \textit{VAR models} provide linear multivariate time-series with known coefficient matrices ($N=128$), serving as a baseline for linear mechanisms. \textit{Lorenz-96 systems} generate chaotic dynamics through coupled differential equations with configurable coupling strength and forcing parameter, testing the model's ability to recover nonlinear feedback loops. \textit{Cluster-Lorenz} extends this by partitioning nodes into spatial clusters with dense intra-cluster and sparse inter-cluster couplings, explicitly probing whether geometric priors improve recovery of spatially structured topologies. \textit{Finance} comprises synthetic financial time-series ($N=40$), providing a non-spatial domain where we assign random virtual coordinates to evaluate robustness when spatial guidance is deliberately adversarial.

\paragraph{Climate dataset.}
We apply GeoDCD to ERA5 reanalysis sea-level pressure (SLP) data at $2.5^\circ \times 2.5^\circ$ spatial resolution, yielding $N=10{,}512$ grid nodes spanning the global surface. Latitude--longitude coordinates serve directly as geometric priors. This dataset provides a unique validation opportunity: atmospheric teleconnection pathways (e.g., SAM, ENSO) are independently established by decades of observational climatology, enabling qualitative verification of recovered causal structures against known physical mechanisms.

\paragraph{Traffic datasets.}
We evaluate on four city-scale traffic networks of increasing magnitude: \textit{San Diego} (SD, $N=716$ sensors from PeMS D8), \textit{Beijing} (BJ, $N=513$ nodes partitioned across ring roads and arterial streets), \textit{Greater Bay Area} (GBA, $N=2{,}352$ sensors spanning 11 municipalities in southern China), and \textit{Greater Los Angeles} (GLA, $N=3{,}834$ nodes from Caltrans detectors). For each dataset we construct sliding windows of length $L=12$ with stride 6, yielding overlapping training sequences. A temporal split of 70\%/15\%/15\% is applied for train/validation/test sets, preserving chronological order to prevent data leakage.

\paragraph{Cross-modal urban dataset.}
The ST-EVCDP dataset covers Shenzhen, China, where 18,061 public EV charging stations are aggregated into 247 traffic analysis zones (TAZs). Each TAZ records both road occupancy and charging energy consumption (kWh), yielding a dual-modality network of $N=494$ nodes (247 traffic $\oplus$ 247 energy). Shared spatial coordinates allow GeoDCD to treat the concatenated system as a unified causal network without architectural modification.

\paragraph{Baseline methods.}
We compare against a comprehensive suite spanning linear and nonlinear approaches: \textit{GVAR} (Global Vector Autoregression), \textit{cMLP}, \textit{cLSTM}, \textit{TCDF}, and \textit{eSRU} as neural baselines; \textit{NAVAR} with both MLP and LSTM encoders; and modern causal discovery methods including \textit{CUTS+}, \textit{UnCLE}, and \textit{Causalformer}. All baselines are re-implemented under identical data preprocessing, architecture constraints (where applicable), and evaluation protocols to ensure fair comparison.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
GeoDCD is implemented in PyTorch with a Causal Transformer encoder ($d_{model}=128$, 4 attention heads, 2 layers), hierarchical pooling reducing $N$ to $K=100$ patches across four levels, and basis decomposition with $N_{\mathrm{basis}}=4$. Training uses Adam optimizer (learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$, cosine annealing over 500 epochs) with L1 sparsity weight $\lambda_{L1}=0.01$. Each experiment is repeated with five independent random seeds.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
All quantitative results are reported across five independent runs with distinct random seeds, ensuring reproducibility of stochastic elements including weight initialization, data shuffling, and dropout. For comparative evaluations on synthetic benchmarks, we report three complementary metrics: (i) AUROC for binary edge detection, (ii) F1-Score balancing precision and recall in causal link identification, and (iii) Structural Hamming Distance (SHD), the minimum number of edge additions, deletions, or reversals required to transform a learned graph into the ground truth. SHD is computed as $d_{\mathrm{SHD}} = d_{\mathrm{add}} + d_{\mathrm{del}} + d_{\mathrm{rev}}$, where each term counts the respective operation count against the reference adjacency matrix.

For real-world road network evaluations, we use RMSE and MAE on 1-hour ahead forecasting as proxies for causal structure quality, which represents a valid proxy given that accurate causal graphs impose stronger constraints on temporal prediction than spurious correlations. All baseline methods are evaluated under identical data splits (70\%/15\%/15\% temporal partition), input normalisation, and look-ahead horizon to ensure comparability. No formal hypothesis testing is performed; effect sizes are quantified through percentage improvement over the best-performing baseline on each dataset, calculated as $(R_{\mathrm{baseline}} - R_{\mathrm{GeoDCD}}) / R_{\mathrm{baseline}} \times 100$, where $R$ denotes the relevant metric (RMSE for forecasting; lower is better).

For the ablation study, we report relative performance changes computed as $(M_{\mathrm{variant}} - M_{\mathrm{full}}) / M_{\mathrm{full}} \times 100$, enabling direct comparison of component contributions across datasets. For robustness analyses under sensor dropout and noise injection, degradation curves are fitted with quadratic models to characterise the rate of performance decay as a function of perturbation magnitude.

\subsection*{Supplementary Notes}

\subsubsection*{Supplementary Note 1: Jacobian Sensitivity Analysis as Neural Granger Causality}

We show that Jacobian-based sensitivity analysis in GeoDCD provides a valid estimator of Granger causal influences in nonlinear, time-varying systems. The proof establishes equivalence between the classical Granger definition and the neural Granger criterion based on partial derivatives of a differentiable predictor, thereby grounding GeoDCD's dynamic causal strength metric in formal causal inference theory.

\paragraph{Granger causality.}
Let \(\Omega_t = \{\mathbf{X}_{s}: s \le t\}\) denote the history of the full system up to time \(t\), and \(\Omega_{-j,t} = \Omega_t \setminus \{X_{j,s}: s \le t\}\) the history excluding variable \(j\). Variable \(X_j\) Granger-causes \(X_i\) if
\[
\mathrm{Var}\bigl(X_{i,t+1} \mid \Omega_t\bigr) < \mathrm{Var}\bigl(X_{i,t+1} \mid \Omega_{-j,t}\bigr),
\]
i.e., including past values of \(X_j\) reduces the one-step-ahead prediction variance of \(X_i\). Equivalently, the optimal predictor \(f_i^*(\Omega_t) = \mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1} \mid \Omega_t]\) depends on at least one past value of \(X_j\). We adopt a first-order Markov assumption (\(\Omega_t = \mathbf{X}_t\)) for notational clarity; extension to higher lags follows by augmenting the input state.

\paragraph{Neural Granger criterion.}
Let \(f_\theta^{(i)}(\mathbf{X}_t)\) be a differentiable predictor (the GeoDCD encoder) trained to minimise mean squared prediction error:
\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{i,t+1} - f_\theta^{(i)}(\mathbf{X}_t)\right)^2\right].
\]
With sufficient capacity and data, \(f_\theta^{(i)} \to \mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1} \mid \mathbf{X}_t]\) almost everywhere. Under this consistency assumption, we define neural Granger causality from \(X_j\) to \(X_i\) by the partial derivative
\[
\frac{\partial f_\theta^{(i)}}{\partial x_{j,t}}(\mathbf{X}_t).
\]

\noindent\textbf{Definition (Neural Granger causal).}
\(X_j\) is neural Granger-causal for \(X_i\) at state \(\mathbf{X}_t\) if \(\frac{\partial f_\theta^{(i)}}{\partial x_{j,t}}(\mathbf{X}_t) \neq 0\) on a set of positive measure.

\paragraph{Equivalence.}

\noindent\textbf{Theorem.}
Under the consistency assumption \(f_\theta^{(i)} = \mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t]\), neural Granger causality is equivalent to the classical Granger definition.

\noindent\textit{Proof.}
\((\Rightarrow)\) If \(\frac{\partial f_\theta^{(i)}}{\partial x_{j,t}} \equiv 0\) almost everywhere, then \(f_\theta^{(i)}\) is independent of \(x_{j,t}\). The conditional expectation remains unchanged when \(x_{j,t}\) is omitted:
\[
\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t] = \mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t}].
\]
Hence \(\mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t) = \mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t})\), and no Granger causality exists.

\((\Leftarrow)\) If the derivative is non-zero on a set of positive measure, \(f_\theta^{(i)}\) varies with \(x_{j,t}\). By the law of total variance,
\[
\mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t}) = \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t) \mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t}\right] + \mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t] \mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t}\right).
\]
The second term is strictly positive when the conditional expectation depends on \(x_{j,t}\). Therefore
\[
\mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_t) < \mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t+1}\mid \mathbf{X}_{-j,t}),
\]
which is the Granger criterion. \(\square\)

\paragraph{Dynamic causal strength in GeoDCD.}
In practice, we compute the Jacobian matrix at each time step \(t\):
\[
\mathbf{J}_t = \frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{X}_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}, \quad \hat{x}_{i,t+1} = f_\theta^{(i)}(\mathbf{X}_t).
\]
The instantaneous causal strength from \(X_j\) to \(X_i\) is the Frobenius norm of the scalar derivative aggregated over feature channels:
\[
\mathcal{S}_{i \leftarrow j}(t) = \left\| \frac{\partial \hat{x}_{i,t+1}}{\partial x_{j,t}} \right\|_F.
\]
A non-zero value of \(\mathcal{S}_{i \leftarrow j}(t)\) indicates that \(X_j\) contributes to predicting \(X_i\) at time \(t\), satisfying the neural Granger criterion. The magnitude reflects the local causal strength.

Thus, GeoDCD's Jacobian analysis provides a mathematically grounded Granger causality estimator that extends naturally to nonlinear, regime-dependent systems without restrictive stationarity or linearity assumptions.
